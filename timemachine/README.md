### Q2 (Difficulties)

<!-- Answer here -->

The biggest challenge in recreating this experience natively is that Adobe Flash Player was officially discontinued in 2020, meaning modern browsers no longer support running Flash content natively at all. This effectively broke access to thousands of games that were never updated to newer technologies like HTML5. Even if the game files still exist somewhere online, they cannot simply be opened and played the way they used to be. This creates a major barrier because the original experience was great just due to how seamless it was. All you needed to do was just clicking a link and instantly started playing.

Another difficulty is finding a safe and reliable way to access these games. Many websites that claim to host old Flash files can be untrustworthy, have questionable ads(if you don't use ad blocking software) or are just unsafe, which makes downloading random files risky. Additionally, even when using preservation tools like Flashpoint, the experience is not exactly the same as playing in a browser. There is an extra layer of setup, and the sense of casually discovering games online is somewhat lost since everything is now accessed through a launcher rather than cruising around the web.

### Q3 (Resources for preservation)

* Were you able to recreate your experience?

* Response:

<!-- Answer here -->
I was able to partially recreate the experience using Flashpoint, a project designed to preserve and provide access to Flash games and animations. Flashpoint works by emulating the environment that Flash content originally ran in. This allowing users to play thousands of archived games offline. This is a major preservation effort that required collecting game files, organizing metadata, and building a system that can safely run them without relying on outdated browser plugins. They do offer 2 versions as well, one called infinity that loads games on demand or a large (2.2TB) archive that contains all the games and can be truly used offline.

While Flashpoint successfully restores access to many games, the experience still feels different. Originally, playing Flash games was spontaneous—you might stumble upon a new game while browsing a website or following links from one game to another. With Flashpoint, the experience is more like browsing a library or database. It is more organized but less exploratory. Additionally, the social context is missing today. That includes sharing games with friends at school or competing for high scores in real time on sites is harder to replicate. Personally one thing I found was missing was blasting early 2010's music which made the experience much more enjoyable for me. Even though the songs can be played today, they do not create the same effect. Despite these differences, the core gameplay and visuals are preserved very accurately, which shows how effective the preservation effort has been.

### Q4 (Benefits of preservation)

<!-- Answer here -->

There is definitely substantial benefit to preserving Flash games because they represent an important part of internet culture and early digital creativity. Flash games were often made by independent developers and hobbyists instead of major studios today. They represent a unique period where the barrier to entry for game creation and sharing was relatively low. Preserving them allows future generations to have the option to study (as a part of history) or experience this form of media, similar to how we preserve old films or early video games. They also have educational value (like this SRC), as they can inspire new developers or push that software should be preserved. Lastly it can show the evolution of game design over time from Tetris to today AAA games like cyber punk 2077.

In terms of cost, the preservation effort seems justified, especially since projects like Flashpoint rely heavily on community contributions rather than large-scale corporate funding. While there are technical challenges—such as maintaining compatibility and storing large amounts of data—the cultural and historical value outweighs these costs. A key one is the nostalgic draw these games contain as they define childhoods of people who are now adults. Without these efforts, an entire category of digital content would have been permanently lost. Once they are lost, it might not be possible to recreate them, making preservation not just worthwhile but necessary.

### Q5 (Comparing with other forms of preservation)

<!-- Answer here -->
Software preservation shares some ethical principles with other more well defined fields like art and architecture, but is complex due to its instability and executability.

As Cesare Brandi argues, conservation should preserve an artifact’s historical and aesthetic truth while minimizing alteration; this is reflected in architecture
    This allows aging but slows it down

Software like Colossal Cave Adventure should be preserved not just as source code but as the experience of those who initially used it. This can be done through proving emulation, documentation, and contextual materials because its cultural value lies in how it is used and experienced.

Preservation should combine traditional conservation ethics (authenticity, minimal intervention) with new practices such as maintaining executability, preserving multiple versions, and documenting user communities


### Q6 (Legal protections and obligation to pay)

* Individual response: 
Protection of intellectual property should remain strong. Software creators invest significant time and money into developing their products, so allowing the free distribution of legacy programs like WordPerfect 5.1 undermines the effort required to create them. In addition, free distribution can discourage future innovation. If companies expect their products to eventually be shared for free, they may be less willing to invest in developing new software. There should also be a clear line between preservation and piracy—distributing software as without permission it is still piracy, even if the intention is to preserve it. Finally, copyright should persist even if companies change or disappear, just as other creative works remain protected long after their creation, meaning software ownership should continue to be respected regardless of the company’s current status.

* Discussion with partner: 
During the discussion we brought up many points for and against digital libraries and preservationists efforts. The first point was the National Film Preservation Act of 1988 which argued that preservation is different from piracy since it is not profit driven.  Another major point that was mentioned is to preserve legacy software that is no longer sold or supported. This point we both agreed was important as once the source software is lost the educational and historical impacts are diminished.

However, the opposing side argued that granting legal protections would weaken intellectual property rights. Software developers invest significant time and money into creating products, and allowing free distribution undermines that investment. It was strongly felt that preservation should also be done long after the peak usage or deprecation of a product so that it would not impact the viability of the software as a product.

In the end we both agreed that there should be legal protections for digital libraries and preservationists efforts. The discussion concluded that these protections should be carefully limited to non-profit, educational, and archival use cases to avoid harming the original creators. This balance allows important historical software to be preserved while still respecting intellectual property rights and encouraging future innovation.
